AI can recognize a screw. It cannot remember that the screw exists once it leaves the frame. That is why AI can design a phone but cannot repair one.

03 · The problem

Repair is a memory problem.

Why now
SEC. 03

A technician disassembles a device and creates a temporary, fragile model of it in their head. The model evaporates when they leave the bench — and with it, the confidence that the job was done right.

01
Lost parts

A dropped screw is a silent failure. The device may still boot, but the next owner inherits a hidden defect.

02
Wrong order

Skipping the battery disconnect can short a board. Order matters, and memory is the only thing enforcing it.

03
No proof

“I fixed it” is not evidence. Shops, insurers, and refurbishers need a record of what was done and what was checked.

04
No transfer

Senior techs carry tacit knowledge. When they leave, it leaves. New techs learn by trial, error, and damaged devices.

The scale
62M t
E-waste generated in 2022 — the fastest-growing waste stream in the world.
22.3%
Formally collected and recycled. The rest is landfill, informal recycling, or hoarded.
10+
US states with right-to-repair laws, plus EU ecodesign rules. Access is expanding; capability is not.
1.9M
Projected unfilled US manufacturing jobs by 2033 — the same skilled-trades squeeze hitting repair.
Who feels it most

Independent repair shops. Thin margins, high turnover, training takes 6–12 months. A single lost screw or wrong-order mistake costs a part, a customer, or a board.

ITAD and refurbishers. They process thousands of devices and need per-device proof for warranty, resale grade, and compliance. Manual inspection does not scale.

DIYers and right-to-repair advocates. They have the manual, but not the memory. They need a second pair of eyes that knows what they just did.

The before / after

Before: “I think I have all the screws. I think I disconnected the battery. I think it’s fine.”

After: “23 of 23 fasteners accounted for. Battery disconnect logged at 14:02. No extra parts. Boot test passed. Passport signed.”

Why now
Perception became a commodity

SAM 3.1 does zero-shot, text-prompted detection and identity-preserving tracking. No training data per device. The eye is finally cheap.

Agents became reliable

Muse Wonder plans over long horizons, calls tools, and recovers from errors. A 20-minute repair loop is now feasible.

Local became viable

Muse Glimmer 30B runs on a single GPU. Private, offline, always-on. The shop can keep customer video on the bench.

“Every repair is a small act of memory. We built the tools, the manuals, and the training — never the memory.” The gap Cairn fills
What the technician actually needs
  • Recall: what came out, from where, and in what order.
  • Order: the one step that must happen before the next.
  • Proof: something to show the customer, the insurer, or the next tech.

08 · Architecture

A loop, not a pipeline.

System design
SEC. 08

The camera is the input. The object graph is the product. The verification loop is the moat.

Deterministic skeleton

The task graph for the demo device is curated. The LLM chooses among valid steps; it does not invent destructive ones. This is how the demo survives.

LLM muscles

Muse Wonder handles language, explanation, adaptation, and interruption recovery. The graph handles truth. Neither replaces the other.

Human is the actuator

Cairn never touches the device. It proposes, observes, and verifies. The human acts. This is a copilot, not a robot — and that is a feature.

The safety kernel

Hard-coded rules the model cannot override: disconnect power before removing components, never pry near a battery, ESD strap required, stop on smoke, heat, or swelling. The LLM can explain a rule; it cannot disable it. This is the difference between a demo and a product.

One loop, 20 seconds
0.0 – 0.4 s

Perceive. Frame in, SAM masks out, graph diffed against the last known state.

0.4 – 1.2 s

Remember. New nodes, edges, and evidence frames are appended. Nothing is overwritten.

1.2 – 1.7 s

Reason. Wonder queries the graph, picks the next valid step, and explains why it matters.

1.7 s +

Act & verify. Voice and overlay respond; the human acts; invariants check the result. Repeat.

The loop is event-driven, not continuous: a change on the bench wakes it, and it sleeps again when the scene is stable.

Latency budget
StageBudget
SAM track< 400 ms / frame
Graph update< 50 ms
Wonder next step< 1.5 s
Voice round trip< 500 ms
Verification< 100 ms
Cached vs. live

Cached: task graph, part labels, SAM prompt templates, tray map, safety rules.

Live: masks, graph updates, next-step reasoning, voice, verification. The cache is what keeps the loop under two seconds.

09 · The object graph

Memory you can audit.

Data model
SEC. 09

Every part is a node. Every relationship is an edge. Every claim has an evidence frame. The graph is the single source of truth for the agent, the UI, and the passport.

Node types
NodeAttributes
Partid, class, label, size, state, location, confidence
Toolid, type, size, location, in_use
Stepid, order, action, preconditions, safety
Observationframe, mask, timestamp, model, confidence
Exceptiontype, severity, resolved, evidence
Edge types
EdgeMeaning
fastensPart is a fastener for another part.
belongs_toPart is part of a subassembly.
connectsCable or connector joins two parts.
requiresStep requires a tool or precondition.
precedesOrdering constraint between steps.
// a part in the graph { "part_id": "p_04", "class": "fastener", "label": "M2×3 hinge screw", "state": "removed", "location": "tray_04", "confidence": 0.97, "evidence": ["frame_01842.jpg"], "edges": [ {"type": "fastens", "to": "hinge_bracket"}, {"type": "requires", "to": "tool_ph0"} ] }
Why a graph, not a table
  • Relationships are the task. “Which screw fastens this bracket?” is a graph query, not a row lookup.
  • Evidence is first-class. Every node points to the frame that justified it. The passport can cite it.
  • State is temporal. Events are appended, never overwritten. You can replay the repair.
  • Exceptions are data. A missing part is a node, not an error message.
The repair state machine
SEALED→ OPENED→ POWER_OFF→ COMPONENTS_OUT→ REASSEMBLING→ VERIFIED ↘ EXCEPTION
Verification invariants
  • Count: every removed part is either reinstalled or explicitly retired.
  • Order: no step runs before its preconditions.
  • Safety: power is off before any component removal.
  • Closure: the tray is empty; no extra parts remain.
  • Function: the device boots and passes the smoke test.
The passport

A signed JSON + PDF bundle: device ID, technician, timestamps, part counts, exception log, evidence frames, and a SHA-256 chain. It is the artifact that turns a repair into a verifiable record — and the seed of the business.

Three queries that run the product
QueryReturnsUsed by
missing_parts(device)[p_04, p_11]Exception banner
next_step(device)“disconnect battery”Voice + overlay

Every event is append-only, so the entire repair can be replayed from the passport.

Credit math: the demo is cheap

A 10-minute teardown at 5 fps ≈ 3,000 frames. At $0.20 / 1k frames, that is $0.60 of SAM tracking. A 20-minute agent session with 200k output tokens is ~$4 of Muse Wonder. The $150 credit is enough for the build, the eval, and ten demo takes — if you cache and use Spark for the fast path.

Build plan
SEC. 11

The plan is ordered by risk, not by feature. Day 1 is a kill test. If perception fails, the idea changes before you write a UI.

Day 1
Kill test. Can SAM 3.1 track 6–8 part classes across a real teardown with acceptable ID switches? If not, change the device, the lighting, or the tray. Do not proceed until this is yes.
Day 2
Object graph. Schema, CRUD, event log. Ingest the first teardown. Prove you can answer “what is missing?” from the graph alone.
Day 3
Agent loop. Muse Wonder + tool calls: query graph, request a SAM prompt, propose the next step. Structured outputs only.
Day 4
Voice + hands-free. Push-to-talk, turn detection, interruption. Tune latency until the conversation feels natural.
Day 5
Verification. Invariants, safety kernel, repair passport. The passport is a product artifact, not a debug log.
Day 6
UI polish. Overlay, tray map, timeline, passport view. Make the state readable in a 1080p screen recording.
Day 7
Local Glimmer. Deploy the always-on watcher; add a “privacy mode” toggle. Document the endpoint and latency.
Day 8
Eval + hardening. Ten recorded sessions; measure recall, ID switches, missing-part latency. Fix the top three failure modes.
Day 9
Demo video. Record five takes; pick the best; add captions; cut to 3:00. Do not leave this to the last day.
Day 10
Buffer + submit. Re-read the rubric; check the checklist; submit early. Keep the backup footage.

The one rule

If a feature does not make the demo more reliable, more visual, or more memorable, it is not in scope. Ten days is enough for one wedge — not for a platform.

The fallback if perception fails

Switch to human-confirmed memory: the user taps the part on the tray, the graph records it. The demo still works — it becomes “memory + verification,” not “autonomous perception.” The product survives; the story survives.

What survives: the object graph, the invariants, the passport, and the return-after-lunch moment. What changes: the input is a tap instead of a mask. Nothing the judges score depends on full autonomy.

The minimum demo
  • One device: a desktop PC or a simple laptop with 6–8 parts.
  • Three interactions: missing screw, wrong order, reassembly check.
  • One artifact: the repair passport.
  • One memory moment: walk away and return.
Design the environment
  • Overhead camera, bright light. Shadows kill tracking.
  • Labeled tray with wells. The tray is the memory; the model just reads it.
  • Slower movements. A demo is not a race; motion blur is the enemy.
  • Larger parts for live. Use screws for the story, bigger parts for the tracking.
What to fake — and what not to

Fake the environment: stage the bench, label the tray, slow the motion, choose a device with clean separations. That is product design. Do not fake the results: the graph, the counts, the passport, and the memory must be real. Judges can smell a staged result; they respect a designed demo.

Glossary

Object permanence: the understanding that objects continue to exist when they are out of sight. Here: a persistent, queryable model of every part across time.

Re-ID: re-identification of an object after occlusion or leaving the frame, preserving its identity.

Invariant: a property that must hold before the repair is considered complete.

Repair passport: a signed record of the repair: parts, steps, exceptions, evidence, and outcome.
